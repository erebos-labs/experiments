A fixed point is not a journey

The exact Wardrop allocation is a stationary comparison: all used routes tie for minimum cost at those fixed flows. It does not specify how a population learns, whether it converges, or how quickly.

Update rule, tie handling, and invariants

Let fᵗ be the three route flows and c(fᵗ) the route costs generated by that day’s flow. Let Mᵗ contain every route whose cost is within 10⁻⁹ × max(1, |minimum cost|) of the minimum. The target bᵗ moves all demand onto Mᵗ, preserving the current proportions among tied winners. If no flow is already on the tied winners, it splits demand evenly among them.

Synchronous: fᵗ⁺¹ = bᵗ
Gradual: fᵗ⁺¹ = (1 − α)fᵗ + αbᵗ, with the displayed constant step 0 < α ≤ 1.

Both updates are convex combinations of nonnegative allocations with the same total demand, so they preserve nonnegativity and conservation. At zero demand every flow remains zero and mean journey time is undefined. A stationary Wardrop allocation stays fixed because all of its used flow is already on tied-fastest routes; near-ties outside the tolerance are not treated as ties.

This is a deliberately stark deterministic best-response recurrence. It omits perception errors, memory beyond one day, habit, heterogeneous information, finite travelers, within-day queues, signals, capacity, departure-time choice, spillback, and induced demand. Constant-step gradual switching can chatter around a discontinuous best-route boundary; a smaller step need not produce a smaller finite-window span or imply convergence.

Primary sources and what this prototype borrows

Watling & Hazelton (2003), The Dynamics and Equilibria of Day-to-Day Assignment Models distinguishes stationary equilibrium from explicit day-to-day processes based on prior experience and emphasizes that discrete-time convergence depends on adjustment rate, cost sensitivity, and the learning rule. See Section 3, PDF pages 10–16, especially equation (6) and the closing summary. Our recurrence is simpler than its perception-learning examples.

Horowitz (1984), The Stability of Stochastic Equilibrium in a Two-Link Transportation Network is a related two-link stochastic stability study. Its title, author and DOI are verified; its detailed equations and results are not reconstructed here. The displayed two-cycle is calculated from our explicitly stated recurrence.

Smith & Wisten (1995), A Continuous Day-to-Day Traffic Assignment Model… concerns continuous day-to-day assignment and existence of dynamic equilibrium, as its verified title states. Our discrete aggregate rule is an authored teaching simplification, not a replication of its model.

Braess, Nagurney & Wakolbinger (2005 translation of 1968) supplies the original affine toy costs (p. 448, equation (3.6), Figure 3) and stationary six-unit example (p. 449, Section 4). The day-to-day recurrence and visual comparison here are new adaptations; no claim of original traffic mathematics is made.

A useful relay model—and where it breaks

1 · Geometry makes the chirp

For steady rolling, Ω² sin θ = 4g/R. Losing tilt therefore raises the precession frequency even while total mechanical energy falls.

2 · Parameters identify the loss

A power-law exponent alone is ambiguous: 4/9 and 1/2 are hard to separate experimentally. Mass, radius, pressure, surface, and a hollow-ring control distinguish competing mechanisms.

3 · Contact is not merely the finale

Contact can be lost intermittently during the motion as well as near rest. A fluid layer, impacts, roughness, deformation, and face friction make a clean sequence an approximation, not an observed universal script.

Synthetic demonstration only: the browser noise sequence is fixed for repeatability, the data obey one exact power law, and the endpoint search is bounded. It shows estimator ambiguity under controlled assumptions; it is not evidence that any particular dissipation mechanism governs a real disk. In a separate offline zero-noise check using 100 log-spaced samples, forcing one power law onto this page’s mixed-loss model over 5–100 ms gives 0.472 with the model endpoint fixed and 0.481 when the endpoint is estimated—even though air supplies 77.01% of modeled loss on average in that window. In a separate seeded ensemble with true exponent 4/9, 80 samples over 20–100 ms, 5% independent Gaussian log-noise, and both log- and linear-spaced sampling, candidate-specific endpoint searches from -19 to +50 ms raised wrong selections from 0.5–1% to 31% (200 repetitions per sampling pattern).

This is a transparent scaling-model visualization, not a contact-dynamics simulation. It numerically integrates the combined loss law in Eq. 5 of Thorne et al. (2026), using Ω² sin θ = 4g/R. Below the violet thin-layer limit, the boundary layer is estimated to fill the disk–table gap, so the plotted continuation is explicitly an extrapolation. The v1 preprint’s displayed boundary-layer thickness and printed transition formula differ by a factor of 4 algebraically; this page uses the direct, more conservative consequence of its displayed thickness convention. That choice affects only the validity marker, not the integrated loss law. The plot stops at θ = 10⁻⁴ rad. Experiments report intermittent and terminal contact loss on apparatus-dependent scales from fractions of a millisecond to tens of milliseconds, with longer intervals in some base-plate conditions; the ideal rolling endpoint is therefore not an observed universal event. μ is a fitted effective rolling coefficient, and changing it is exploratory rather than a material prediction.

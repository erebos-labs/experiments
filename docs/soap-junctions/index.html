Why 120°, and when it disappears

This is the classical Fermat–Torricelli point for three terminals, or three-terminal Steiner-tree problem. For the broader planar shortest-network setting, see Gilbert & Pollak, Steiner Minimal Trees (1968).

Inside the triangle, moving the junction changes three lengths at once. Vector balance plus convexity determines the minimum: the three equal-magnitude unit vectors along the branches must sum to zero, which requires 120° separation.

But topology has a boundary case. If one boundary angle reaches 120°, no interior point can maintain the three-way balance. The shortest network collapses to that corner in a sharp topological transition. This simple model explains the local force balance; it does not simulate film curvature, pressure differences, drainage, or full three-dimensional foam dynamics.

The energy gap fades first. In the symmetric sweep, if δ is the shortfall from 120° in radians, the junction’s distance from the corner is approximately proportional to δ, while its length advantage over the collapsed network is proportional to δ². The preferred geometry can still move visibly even when the energetic preference is already shallow.

Where the ideal rule meets real foam

The 120° angle is exact for an equilibrium dry junction with equal film tensions. Finite wet borders require a different geometric model; unequal tensions, surfactant gradients, disjoining pressure, or motion can produce departures. These local angle laws also do not guarantee global stability: small faces can still collapse. The famous 109.47° rule for four Plateau borders is a distinct three-dimensional tetrahedral singularity—not a four-branch version of this planar construction.

  • Joseph Plateau’s 1873 experimental synthesis: volume I, especially §§173, 181, and 184–185, and volume II (French, public-domain scans).
  • Jean E. Taylor’s 1976 classification of soap-film-like minimal singularities: Annals of Mathematics 103, 489–539.
  • A modern treatment that distinguishes dry films from wet Plateau borders: Maggi, Novack & Restrepo (2023).
  • Physical limits: Stavans (1990) observed no angle deviation in thin, nearly constant borders; Neimark & Vignes-Adler (1995) modeled several-degree departures in black films.
  • Local rules versus global stability: Hutzler et al. (2008) report spontaneous collapse of sufficiently small triangular faces.

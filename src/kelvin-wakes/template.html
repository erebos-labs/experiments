Geometry selects possible directions.

In the ideal deep-water model, group velocity is half the phase velocity. Following each stationary wave packet gives an outer ray envelope at arcsin(1/3) = 19.47°, independent of speed.

The disturbance weights those directions.

A smooth Gaussian pressure patch excites wavelengths unequally. At high speed the dominant crests move inward. Slope and Laplacian emphasize shorter waves, selecting still smaller angles.

Model scope: small linear waves, infinite depth, no surface tension, no viscosity, and a circular Gaussian pressure patch. Real hulls, finite depth, currents, breaking waves, lighting, and camera response can change the picture. Finite-distance tails may extend beyond the geometric envelope.

Model, resolution, and primary sources

Use Fr = U / √(g b), with b the pressure-patch size and λg = 2π U²/g. The Fourier pressure spectrum is P̂(K) = exp[−K²/(4π²)], where K = k b. This follows the linear far-field residue model in Darmon, Benzaquen & Raphaël (2014), Eq. 2.8. The near-field contribution is omitted.

The Gaussian spectrum is positive at every finite wavelength. At Fr = 2 its value at the geometric cusp wavevector is about 0.9964, so the narrow maximum does not require long waves to be forbidden.

The plotted statistic is the largest absolute height, gradient magnitude, or absolute spatial Laplacian over 49 radii from 10 to 11 λg, sampled every 0.125°. Derivatives are evaluated in the integral, not estimated from a picture. Peak positions retain finite-window and sampling sensitivity; they are not measurements of real ships. Normalization removes absolute amplitude differences between curves.

With t = tan θ and q = 1+t², the implemented complex integral is proportional to ∫ q Fr⁻⁴ exp[−q²/(4π²Fr⁴)] exp[i 2πr (cos φ − t sin φ) √q] dt. Its imaginary part is the signed height convention used here; an overall common factor is omitted. Integration is bounded by a Gaussian tail exponent of 30 and a conservative phase-step limit.

For high speed and small angle, a derivative of order m weights the dominant wave by Kᵐ. Differentiating its envelope gives φpeak ≈ Cm / Fr, where Cm = 1 / [√π (8(5+4m))¼]. The slope expression is the leading single-branch approximation; the plotted gradient is evaluated directly.

This asymptotic expression is unsuitable at low speed or near the cusp. Separate stationary-phase branches become singular as they merge at 19.47°; the plotted data use quadrature through that region instead.

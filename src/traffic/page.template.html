02 / THE CATCH

Stable needn’t mean best

Selfish routing balances the times of used routes; no tiny driver can gain by switching alone. Coordinated routing minimizes the sum of everyone’s travel time. It can assign different journey times and needs cooperation or incentives. A lower average can still leave some drivers worse off.

03 / THE ESCAPE

More choice still can help

Read the model, assumptions, and exact calculation

The demand stays fixed when the shortcut is toggled. Roads are directed; every stream goes from S to T. All drivers are identical and infinitesimally small (a stationary, nonatomic Wardrop model). Flows are continuous, so fractional allocations are allowed. The animation is decorative; controls immediately recompute an analytic solution.

The two congestible edges have time a × flow, the outer edges b + k × flow, and the shortcut s + h × flow. If upper and lower route flows are both u and shortcut-route flow is z, then 2u + z = q. Symmetry and convexity give equal upper/lower flow in these two models.

Selfish: z = clamp((2(b − s) − (a − k)q)/(a + k + 2h), 0, q).
Coordinated: z = clamp((b − s − (a − k)q)/(a + k + 2h), 0, q).
Shortcut closed: z = 0; u = q/2.

The selfish formula equalizes used-route travel times, with inequalities for unused routes. The coordinated formula minimizes C(z) = a(q+z)²/2 + b(q−z) + k(q−z)²/2 + sz + hz². Mean time is C/q for q > 0. At q = 0 there is no mean journey; plotted lines show only their limiting values.

Flow conservation at every node

One numerical trap from the original paper was reproduced separately: greedily adding demand in 100,000 tiny batches, without revising earlier assignments, left 3.6364 flow units on the shortcut at demand 20. Its mean was 189.7524 and its switching incentive 73.6366 time units; the exact equilibrium has no shortcut flow and mean 160. More batches did not repair the frozen early choices. That loading heuristic is neither a solver used here nor a model of actual drivers.

These are toy costs, not measured roads. No signals, spillback, junction capacity, heterogeneous trips, induced demand, or route-learning dynamics are modeled. This experiment cannot recommend opening or closing a real road.

Primary sources and what has been adapted

Braess, Nagurney & Wakolbinger (2005), On a Paradox of Traffic Planning: English translation of the 1968 paper. Sections 3–4 supply the original costs and the 6-unit example. Section 6 warns that adding traffic greedily without revising earlier assignments need not converge to the equilibrium. Braess first emphasizes minimizing the worst used-route time; our coordinated control explicitly minimizes the mean (also discussed in his paper).

Easley & Kleinberg (2010), Networks, Crowds, and Markets, chapter 8: the simpler 45-minute / x÷100 example with 4,000 drivers. Here we rescale 1,000 drivers to one flow unit and use continuous stationary flows rather than individual finite players.

Anna & Ladimer Nagurney (2020), The Braess Paradox: author-hosted account of Wardrop equilibrium, system optimization, and demand dependence. The closed-form demand curves here are derived from the displayed toy costs; they are not empirical traffic estimates.

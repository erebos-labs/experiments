Can noise help a detector?

A weak wave never reaches a fixed threshold. Add noise, and its rhythm can become visible. Change the score, and the best noise level changes too.

Break the story

Free the threshold

Try “Retune: threshold 0.” With no noise and a nonzero wave, this idealized comparator identifies positive versus negative phase perfectly. Noise helped a constrained readout that was throwing useful measurements away.

Keep the raw measurements

Here the original measurement is an exact sine wave or zero. A raw-data detector can already distinguish them perfectly. With Gaussian noise added, the raw likelihood-ratio test loses power as noise grows.

For a random sample’s phase label L, the chain is L → clean X → X + noise → binary Y. Independent noise cannot raise information above what X had. The noisy binary output can still beat the noiseless binary output: those are two different lossy readouts of X.

Counterexample: a louder harmonic, worse detection

Start with an already noisy Gaussian measurement (SD0.8). Hold those original readings fixed and add independent dither with SD0.6. Total SD becomes1.0. At A0.25 and threshold1, the expected signed harmonic grows from0.05746 to0.06049, while phase-label information falls from0.00993 to0.00793 bits.

A paired2048-record control at N256 found a mean harmonic increase0.003073 [95% mean interval0.001819–0.004327] and mean plug-in information decrease−0.001924 [−0.002406–−0.001441]. No extra measurement is acquired within a pair.

In the separate exact N16 presence test, at identical5% false alarms, the optimal binary detector falls from13.55% to12.01% detection. Raw-data optimal detection falls from22.33% to17.42%. The louder harmonic is real; treating it as universally better detection is the mistake.

Model, metrics, uncertainty and finite-sample limits

One cycle has 64 midpoint phase samples. A record contains 1, 4 or 16 complete cycles. Changing sample count changes duration and the number of independent draws; it is not a free resampling improvement. All conditions compared at a setting use the same count.

Clean input: xᵢ = A sin(2π(i + ½)/64). Noisy input: xᵢ + σzᵢ with independent standard-normal zᵢ. Output: yᵢ = 1[xᵢ + σzᵢ > θ]. There is no hysteresis, relaxation time, memory, measurement error before injection or refractory period. A mathematical ideal, not a hardware, neuron or biological experiment.

Phase accuracy classifies the sign of a reference sine: hit rate is P(y = 1 | positive phase), phase false rate is P(y = 1 | negative phase), accuracy = (1 + hit − false)/2. It is not the same task as signal presence. Phase-label information is the mutual information between that balanced binary label and one output sample. For this diagnostic, choose a sample time uniformly from the phase grid and withhold its index from the information calculation. The phase label is already known to an observer with the reference clock, so this is a dependency score, not discovery of an unknown phase. It is not information about the full waveform or an unknown signal.

The signed projection is 2Σyᵢsin(phaseᵢ)/N. Fourier magnitude combines its sine and cosine components and is positive even under pure noise. The analytic curves average Gaussian exceedance probabilities over the 64 phase bins. Simulated curves use 32 fixed seed batches with 10th–90th percentile shading across records. Analytic information uses probabilities; the plug-in estimate from a short record is biased upward. The table shows the latter. An exact binomial calculation at SD0.8, threshold1 gives an expected null plug-in value of 0.002875 bits for N256 despite true information zero (0.012711 bits for N64; 0.000708 bits for N1024).

Mulberry32 pseudorandom generator with Box–Muller Gaussian draws; reproducible seeds, not cryptographic or physical randomness. The probabilistic model treats the injected realization as unobserved by the detector. The seed allows replication; none of the detector rules subtract its known draws. Giving a detector the realized noise as side information would change the information problem. Signal and null use distinct seed families. Across noise strengths each family reuses its underlying standardized draws to reduce comparison variance. Curves and their pointwise intervals are therefore correlated. Mean intervals are approximate; they do not account for choosing a peak after looking across the sweep.

Known phase/frequency are supplied to the projection and raw likelihood-ratio tests. There is no frequency search or multiple-frequency correction. Discrete independent noise is not pointwise continuous-time white noise. A finer grid with fresh independent samples changes the information budget. A separate stationary AR(1) stress test, at unchanged marginal variance and N256, raised false alarms from 5.3% to 21.7% at lag correlation0.9 when the IID cutoff was reused; matched correlated-null calibration returned 4.5% false alarms with 18.0% detection. These are finite-seed results, not part of the IID curves. No continuous-time crossing rate is reported.

Primary sources and what this bench does not reproduce
  • Gingl, Kiss & Moss (1995): non-dynamical stochastic resonance. The physical experiment uses directional level crossings and band-limited noise. This sampled occupancy comparator does not replicate their pulse process or spectral normalization.
  • Kosko & Mitaim (2003): noisy threshold neurons. Discrete threshold model and mutual information for bipolar Bernoulli inputs. This bench uses a sinusoid and groups its phase by sign.
  • Chen et al. (2007): fixed detectors. Primary repository abstract distinguishes detection probability and false-alarm constraints. Only the primary repository abstract is used; full-text methods are not assessed.
  • Polyanskiy & Wu: data-processing inequalities. The Markov-chain bound explains why a better constrained readout does not create measurement information.

This is an educational numerical test of established ideas. No scientific discovery or empirical sensory benefit is claimed.

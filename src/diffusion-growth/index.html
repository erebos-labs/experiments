THE MECHANISM

Open gaps can
still be hard to reach.

A walker approaching from outside tends to meet an exposed arm before it enters a deep gap. Each new tip changes access for the next particle. Small accidents become lasting branches.

Eden removes this competition for access: every empty site next to the cluster is equally likely to fill. Both rules are random. Their randomness makes very different shapes.

Rules, limits, and primary sources

Exact toy rules

Start with one occupied square-lattice cell at (0,0). Launch one walker at a uniform random angle on the source circle, rounding to a lattice cell. Choose each of four cardinal steps with probability ¼. On an empty site adjacent to the cluster, attempt to stick.

For p < 1, a rejected walker takes another step. A step into the cluster bounces in place. Every such iteration may attempt sticking again. Escapes and walkers reaching 100,000 moves restart from the source. A run stops at its chosen mass, 100 million moves, or the finite-domain limit; the reason is displayed.

A different rejection rule gives a different model. If every rejected walker is relaunched independently and p is constant, accepting a first hit only thins the same spatial distribution: p cancels after conditioning on acceptance. That rule slows attachment but does not reproduce the local exploration used here.

R is the greatest distance from the seed to an occupied cell. Source radius is at least 5. Escape radius is at least source+5. Storage spans −512 to +512 on each axis; occupied cells must lie from −511 to +511; probes stop at 20 million moves or 1,500 hits. Frame work budgets change speed only. A Mulberry32 seed fixes the trajectory; probe randomness is separate.

What this does not establish

This is a seeded visual toy with bounded numerical checks, not a calibrated physical simulation. It has no particle size in meters, chemical kinetics, surface tension, field-driven drift, material relaxation, or physical clock.

Four-way steps impose a square lattice. Meakin’s 1986 study reports sensitivity to lattice anisotropy in much larger simulations. The enclosing circles, contact convention, finite mass, and walker cap can affect results. We do not fit or certify a fractal dimension. The clusters look like trees but may contain lattice cycles.

Reading

Witten & Sander (1981) introduced DLA. Their expanded primary study (1983), §II discusses screening, Eden growth, launch/escape circles, and partial sticking.

In his 1993 retrospective, Witten says DLA did not explain the iron-colloid process that inspired it. Resemblance alone is an insufficient test of mechanism.

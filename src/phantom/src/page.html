Two ears.
More than one place.

A sound can seem to occupy a place where no speaker exists. The trick is to supply cues compatible with that place. But one cue can fit several places at once.

Explore the geometry silently. Optional audio is muted by default. This is a model of signals, not a listening test. On narrow screens, diagrams can scroll sideways.

02

A movement becomes another measurement

At rest the candidates agree. When you turn toward A, its delay moves toward zero. B’s delay changes the other way near the starting pose.

Wightman and Kistler’s 1999 experiments found far fewer front–back confusions when head movements were encouraged. Their experiment also tested source motion; the details matter. Read the research abstract ↗

Here we test only whether two ideal measurements differ. We do not model how a person weighs those cues.

05

What the model can establish

Calculated here

A single delay cannot uniquely identify a direction. Two nonparallel receiver poses resolve the horizontal front/back pair in ideal noiseless geometry when the source stays fixed and pose changes are known. Small turns are poorly conditioned when timing errors are added. Yaw alone leaves a vertical mirror ambiguity.

Not measured here

Perceived location, externalization, localization accuracy, device output level, and individual differences. No human listening study was run.

Model contract

τ = tL − tR
τ(θ, ψ, ε) = (d / c) sin(θ − ψ) cos ε
d = 0.18 m · c = 343 m/s
|τ| ≤ 524.8 μs

θ: world azimuth, positive to the right. ψ: yaw, positive to the right. ε: elevation. Far-field plane wave; unobstructed point receivers; equal channel amplitudes. The numerical maximum belongs to this model, not a human head.

Two corrections that make the model more precise

Elevation magnitude is information. Given exact yaw measurements of a stationary source, the recovered horizontal components determine cos(elevation). A +60° and a −60° source still agree, but they can differ from a source on the horizon. “Yaw provides no elevation information” would be incorrect.

Source motion changes the inverse problem. A fixed source at world azimuth 30° has the same ideal delay throughout a yaw turn as a second source following world azimuth 150° + 2 × yaw. The moving alternative mirrors the first direction relative to the head at every instant. A head turn alone cannot uniquely locate an unconstrained moving source.

A related primary experiment by Brimijoin & Akeroyd (2012) used a source moving at twice the head rotation rate. Seven listeners reported front/back reversals whose strength depended on the signal spectrum. The mathematical construction here is a simplified instance of that established geometry.

The counterexample tests an assumption, not human listening performance. Real direction-specific spectral cues and expectations about motion are outside this delay-only model. See README.md and the reproducible counterexample checks accompanying this file. This is static-pose geometry, not a time-domain moving-source or Doppler simulation.

Why two measurements help, and where they still fail

For sources on the horizon, write A = d/c, m₀ = A sin θ and mh = A sin(θ − h). Then sin θ = m₀/A and cos θ = (m₀ cos h − mh)/(A sin h). Together they recover horizontal azimuth with atan2. At h = 0° or 180° the denominator vanishes. Near those poses, timing error is magnified.

In 3D, yaw-only measurements determine horizontal components x and y. The unit-vector condition gives z² = 1 − x² − y², leaving the sign of height unresolved. This is an identifiability result for the model, not a general statement that people cannot locate height.

At finite range the delay surfaces are hyperboloids; the cone description is a far-field limit. Front/back reflection still preserves both path lengths in the symmetric two-point geometry.

What changes if the head is a rigid obstacle?

A companion calculation replaces the empty space between receivers with a perfectly rigid sphere of radius 9 cm, with diametrically opposite receivers. Its frequency response changes both level and phase. Yet front/back mirrors still produce the same pair of complex responses: every point on an equal-incidence ring remains equivalent. More cue values do not add a missing coordinate when they share the same symmetry. This exact result requires the stated receiver placement: moving both receivers rearward on the sphere breaks the front/back spectral match in a companion sensitivity check, while the above/below mirror remains.

Sources and reproducibility

Primary research is linked below. Ideal calculations, published participant results and inference are distinct. The 1999 source is abstract-only; no replication of a participant experiment or listening result is claimed.

  1. Wightman & Kistler (1990), Hearing in three dimensions: Sound localization. Virtual-source synthesis, headphone correction, and the distinction between lateralization and localization. Report PDF.
  2. Wightman & Kistler (1999), Resolution of front–back ambiguity in spatial hearing by listener and source movement. Experimental head/source movement conditions.
  3. Macpherson & Middlebrooks (2002), Listener weighting of cues for lateral angle. Cue weights depend on the stimulus; no universal delay-only rule.
  4. W3C, Web Audio API. Browser audio buffers and playback lifecycle.
  5. Sridhar & Choueiri (2021), Optimal series truncation of the rigid-sphere head-related transfer function. Far-field series formulation and numerical convergence issues.
  6. McAnally & Martin (2014), Sound localization with head movement. Absolute-elevation information and experiment-specific motion conditions.
  7. McLachlan et al. (2023), Dynamic spectral cues during small head movements. Static versus dynamic spectra; seven participants and 10° acoustic rotations.
  8. Langendijk & Bronkhorst (2002), Contribution of spectral cues to human sound localization, author-uploaded primary text. Spectral-band perturbations and individual cues.
  9. Kim, Barnett-Cowan & Macpherson (2013), Integration of auditory input with motion information. Preliminary active/passive/counter-rotation study, with limitations in the original discussion.
